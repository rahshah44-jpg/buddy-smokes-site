THE LEADER

Buddy Smokes

Buddy Smokes wasn’t created in a boardroom or dreamed up by some high-powered marketing team—he came home with Sekai Rasha Lewis. Years ago, Sekai walked through the door excited about a photograph he had taken of cannabis inside a plastic bag, an ordinary image that somehow had a face, a personality, and something to say. That photograph became the seed of an idea between father and son: what if cannabis itself could speak? What if it could tell its own story instead of constantly having its story told by stereotypes: celebrities, politicians, corporations, critics, and the justice system? From that idea a half pound of weed became Buddy Smokes—a blunt, funny, intelligent, unapologetic voice for a plant whose history stretches back hundreds of thousands of years. But before Sekai and I could fully realize that vision together, he tragically passed away following a motorcycle accident in 2025. What began as one of our creative ideas suddenly became something much more personal. Bringing Buddy to life became part of carrying Sekai's creativity forward. Today, Buddy's purpose is bigger than simply entertaining people: through music, comedy, interviews, culture, and The Cannabis Campaign, he challenges misinformation, confronts hypocrisy, questions the laws and industries built around cannabis, and gives the plant something it has never had—a voice of its own.

Sekai captured the image and together we saw the character. Now Buddy carries the vision forward.

THE BALANCED ONE

Dream

Dream—better known as “Blue Dream” for those unmistakable dreamy blue eyes—is the balance in The Resin Team, the one who can cool everybody down without ever fading into the background. And balance is literally in her blood: Blue Dream is generally recognized as a sativa-dominant hybrid born from Blueberry and Haze, two very different sides of the cannabis family—Blueberry bringing the heavier, calmer indica influence and Haze bringing the energetic, cerebral sativa side. Dream likes to say she inherited Mama Blueberry’s calm and Daddy Haze’s head in the clouds: one taught her when to slow things down, the other taught her when to lift everybody higher. Maybe that's why, when Buddy gets too confrontational, Kush gets too street, Sour gets too caught up in himself, or Noid gets too damn serious, Dream knows exactly how to bring everybody back to center. She’s beautiful, confident and smooth, but don't confuse softness with weakness—Dream is proof that being a hybrid doesn't mean being caught between two worlds; sometimes it means you were born with the best of both.

THE POLLINATOR

Sour Diesel

Sour Diesel, aka “The Pollinator,” is the youngest member of The Resin Team—a rebellious young singer and rapper whose sound, like his genetics, refuses to stay in one lane. Sour D’s real-world lineage is somewhat mysterious, but it’s widely believed to descend from Chemdawg ’91 and Super Skunk, producing a sativa-dominant hybrid known for being energetic, cerebral, talkative, and unmistakably loud. Diesel carries that same mixed blood into his music, blending R&B, hip-hop, soul, melodic rap, and touches of rock into something that doesn’t quite belong to any one genre. Even his eyes refuse to match—one gray, the other tan—almost like nature stamped him with a reminder that different sides can exist in the same body and still make something dope. He’s the young heartthrob of the crew: smooth, mysterious, naturally charismatic, and usually the one getting the loudest reaction when The Resin Team hits the stage. But behind the pretty-boy appeal is a young rebel with very little interest in playing industry politics. Diesel says what everybody else is thinking, whether it helps him or gets him in trouble, and if the truth is sour, he’s still going to give it to you unfiltered.

THE RED EYE

Kush Th3 OG

Kush Th3 OG—aka “The Red Eye”—is a hard-to-read hybrid with roots that stretch from the mountains of Central Asia to Thailand and deep into American cannabis culture. Born from Chemdawg, his streetwise, loud-mouthed father with that unmistakable diesel funk; Lemon Thai, his sharp, energetic mother who brought the citrus, creativity and cerebral edge; and the old-world Hindu Kush bloodline that gave him his heavy body, resilience and unmistakable Kush character, Kush comes from a family that learned early how to survive underground. When he was young, his family left the West during an era when cannabis prohibition could turn the plant they came from into a reason for arrest, incarceration and a ruined future. They headed East, where Kush grew up learning how to move quietly, read people and never forget where he came from. Years later, legalization changed the landscape, but it didn't erase his memory. Today, Kush Th3 OG keeps one foot in the streets that raised him and the other in the legal dispensaries of the West Coast that now sell openly what his family once had to hide. He doesn't talk much about those years—but the permanently red eyes earned him the name “The Red Eye,” and when Kush does speak, everybody on The Resin Team knows there’s usually a story behind what he says.

THE WILD CARD

Noid

Noid is an all-natural, sharp-tongued strain whose age is unknown but whose wisdom stretches far beyond recorded history. Sarcastic, pugnacious and unapologetically outspoken, he has spent countless years studying the sun, rain, soil and the laws of Nature—knowledge no laboratory could ever reproduce. Noid refuses to conform to the plastic-covered world around him and is openly critical of anything artificial, especially growth chemicals that interfere with the natural life of the plant. You can always expect him to say something unexpected, because remaining silent has never been in his nature. Whether anyone agrees with him or not, Noid will speak—and when he does, even the wisest know to listen.

CULTURE

The Culture Was Never Just a Boys’ Club

By Dream | The Resin Team (TRT)

For years, popular culture sold one narrow picture of the cannabis consumer: a young man on a couch, smoke in the room, ambition somewhere outside the frame. That image was repeated so often that people started treating it like history. It was never history. It was marketing, stereotype and selective memory. Women have always been part of cannabis culture—as healers, patients, growers, caregivers, activists, artists and entrepreneurs. What is changing now is not their presence. What is changing is their visibility.

CNN’s Weed 8: Women and Weed, reported by Dr. Sanjay Gupta, arrived at the right moment. The documentary examines the growing number of women using cannabis and the women helping shape the legal market. Gupta travels to Oklahoma, where medical cannabis created an unlikely green rush in a politically conservative state. The setting matters. It shows that cannabis does not belong to one coast, one political identity or one type of person. It also shows women moving from private consumption into public leadership.

That shift challenges more than the old “stoner” stereotype. It challenges the assumptions placed on women. A man who uses cannabis may be treated as relaxed, creative or rebellious. A woman—especially a mother or professional—can still be judged as irresponsible, unstable or unfit. That double standard has forced many women to keep their use private even when cannabis helps them manage pain, sleep problems, menopause symptoms or the pressure of daily life. Visibility changes that conversation, but visibility alone does not guarantee respect.

The business side contains another contradiction. Women are among the fastest-growing groups of cannabis consumers, yet leadership and investment do not automatically follow consumer growth. The industry uses phrases such as “female-focused” and “women’s wellness,” but putting soft colors on a package is not the same as placing women in ownership, science, cultivation, product development and policy. If women are valuable enough to target as customers, they are valuable enough to help control the companies, research and rules shaping the market.

Representation also changes product design. A market built around outdated assumptions may overlook how body composition, hormones, medications and life stages can influence a person’s experience. Women deserve products developed from evidence rather than scaled-down versions of products originally marketed to men. They also deserve clinical conversations in which they can describe cannabis use without expecting shame or dismissal. The point is not to declare one universal “women’s experience.” It is to recognize that a large and varied population has been underserved by both research and public conversation.

There is also a cultural question hiding underneath the statistics: What happens when cannabis becomes part of ordinary self-care? Cannabis is increasingly discussed alongside sleep routines, yoga, recovery, intimacy and wellness. That can help remove stigma, but it can also sanitize the plant’s history. Cannabis did not suddenly become respectable when companies discovered women with disposable income. Patients, underground growers and activists carried this movement through decades of arrests, ridicule and misinformation. The new culture should welcome women without erasing the people who made the moment possible.

The changing gender balance also expands the kinds of stories cannabis media can tell. Women are asking different questions about dosage, hormones, pregnancy, aging, safety and the medical evidence behind product claims. Those questions deserve research, not slogans. A responsible industry should not market cannabis as a cure-all simply because a new audience is listening. Respect means giving women accurate information, transparent testing and room to make informed decisions—not replacing an old stereotype with a polished sales pitch.

Media has a responsibility here as well. Stories should move beyond novelty headlines announcing that women use cannabis. The more useful reporting asks which women have access, who is being studied, whose businesses receive investment and who still faces the harshest judgment. Race, income, age, disability and geography all affect the freedom to participate openly. A wealthy wellness customer and a low-income medical patient may use the same plant while facing entirely different consequences.

For The Resin Team, balance has never meant silence. It means holding two truths at once. Women are helping move cannabis into a broader and more honest cultural space. At the same time, the industry must prove that it values them as more than a demographic. The “grass ceiling” will not disappear through branding. It disappears when women have authority, access to capital, scientific attention and the freedom to speak about cannabis without being reduced to a label.

Women did not just arrive in cannabis culture. The culture is finally learning to see them.

INDUSTRY

America Has an Opinion About Cannabis—Several of Them, Actually

By Noid | The Resin Team (TRT)

Human beings do enjoy declaring a debate finished while continuing to argue about every part of it. Cannabis is legal here, illegal there, medical over here, recreational two miles down the road and suddenly criminal again when somebody crosses the wrong line on a map. Lawmakers keep flipping the switch back and forth as if the plant changes depending on which state trooper is standing nearby. The plant stays the same. The laws are the part that cannot make up their minds.

According to a January 2026 Pew Research Center survey, 55 percent of American adults said marijuana should be legal for both medical and recreational use. Another 33 percent supported medical use only. Just 11 percent wanted it completely illegal. That means total prohibition has been rejected by nearly nine out of ten Americans—and yet politicians still drag it around like an old couch nobody wants but nobody has the nerve to throw away.

Ask whether legalization is good for local economies, and most people see a benefit. Pew reported that 52 percent considered recreational legalization good for local economies, compared with 17 percent who considered it bad. Ask about criminal justice, and 42 percent said legalization makes the system fairer, while 18 percent said it makes it less fair.

The plant could have told you that. Prohibition was never handed out evenly. The same amount of cannabis could mean a warning for one person, a ruined future for another and a business opportunity for somebody with better lawyers and richer friends. Now some of the same governments that spent decades arresting people for cannabis are collecting taxes from it. Apparently, putting a receipt on the bag changed everybody’s morals.

So Americans can agree that cannabis should be legal while disagreeing about nearly every reason why. Fine. Debate is healthy. But stop using public disagreement as an excuse to keep the law confused. If lawmakers need more time to decide what they believe, they should take that time without turning ordinary people into criminals while they think about it.

“Do you support legalization?” is too small a question for a subject this large. One person may support access for patients but oppose aggressive commercial advertising. Another may support adult possession while worrying about impaired driving or high-potency products. Someone else may view legalization primarily as a correction to decades of unequal policing. The word legalization contains medical care, criminal justice, taxes, business, parenting, agriculture, home cultivation, public consumption and personal freedom.

Of course the answers are complicated. That does not mean the rules have to be ridiculous.

Age and politics still shape public opinion. Younger adults generally show stronger support for recreational legalization than older adults. Party differences remain, although complete opposition is now a minority position. The more important change is that cannabis no longer sits neatly inside one political camp. Conservative states operate medical programs. Veterans advocate for access. Parents of sick children have challenged old drug-war assumptions. Small businesses push back against restrictions imposed by state regulators and powerful corporations.

The plant keeps growing through every category people build to contain it. Maybe people should stop blaming the plant because their boxes are too small.

Public opinion also depends on what people imagine when they hear the word cannabis. Some picture a patient managing seizures or chronic pain. Some picture smoke around children. Some picture tax revenue, impaired drivers, dispensaries, farmers, corporations or arrests. Those are completely different subjects being squeezed into the same question.

If a policy discussion uses only the word marijuana without defining the activity—medical treatment, personal possession, retail sales, home cultivation or public consumption—the result may look more unified than the public truly is. Then lawmakers take that muddy answer and write another muddy law. Afterward, everybody acts surprised when consumers, police officers, patients and businesses cannot understand it.

Language influences those reactions. Marijuana, cannabis, medical treatment, adult use and drug use can describe overlapping realities while producing very different emotional responses. Polling questions are not automatically dishonest because wording matters, but readers should examine exactly what was asked.

Support for legal possession is not necessarily support for unrestricted corporate sales. Concern about public smoking is not opposition to medical access. Support for regulation is not permission to treat every consumer like a suspect. These differences matter—especially when politicians selectively quote whichever number supports the law they already wanted to pass.

The legal industry should also pay attention. Broad public support is not permanent permission to behave carelessly. Companies that market recklessly, make unsupported health claims, hide behind confusing labels or ignore community concerns will strengthen opposition. Regulators who respond by piling restriction on top of restriction will do the same from the opposite direction.

One side treats cannabis like a miracle product. The other still treats it like it crawled out of the ground carrying a weapon. Neither position is serious.

Public confidence requires honest labeling, reliable testing, reasonable safeguards and laws ordinary people can understand without hiring an attorney. It also requires consistency. A person should not be considered responsible in one jurisdiction and criminal in the next for possessing the same plant in the same amount for the same reason.

Local experience will influence national opinion. A resident who sees responsible stores, accurate labels and cannabis tax revenue used transparently may view legalization differently from someone surrounded by confusing rules, unlicensed shops and no visible community benefit. Policy is not judged only in legislatures. It is judged on neighborhood blocks, in doctors’ offices, inside dispensaries and around kitchen tables.

The survey’s clearest message is not that every American suddenly loves cannabis. They do not. It is that total prohibition has lost the argument. What replaces it remains unsettled because too many lawmakers are trying to keep one foot in legalization and the other planted in the drug war.

That halfway position is not caution. It is cowardice dressed as policy.

Americans want access, but many also want reasonable boundaries. They recognize economic and criminal-justice benefits while remaining concerned about health and safety. Those concerns deserve honest answers, scientific research and regulations that address actual harm. They do not justify laws that keep changing cannabis from medicine to merchandise to contraband depending on where someone stands.

Nature does not conduct opinion polls. People do. Nature also does not redraw state lines, rewrite criminal codes or pretend a plant becomes more dangerous when it crosses a border.

If cannabis policy is ever going to mature, the conversation must move beyond the tired choice between “legal” and “illegal.” The harder question is this: What kind of legal system are we growing—and how many more people will be punished while politicians keep changing their minds?

COMMENTARY

We Legalized It. So Why Does It Still Feel Broken?

By Buddy Smokes | The Resin Team (TRT)

The legalization of cannabis was supposed to end the nonsense. Stop arresting people over the plant. Replace underground sales with regulated and tested products. Create legitimate businesses, jobs and tax revenue. Give patients reliable access. Repair some of the damage caused by the War on Drugs. That was the pitch. That was what legalization promised to deliver.

Instead, many states built legal markets so expensive, restricted and uneven that the people who carried cannabis culture through prohibition are often locked outside of the industry they helped create.

The first problem is the continuing split between state permission and federal prohibition. A cannabis business can follow every rule issued by its state and still face federal restrictions involving banking, transportation, taxes and interstate commerce. A product can be legal on one side of a state line and illegal on the other. A business owner can receive a license from the state while still being treated differently from nearly every other legal business in the country.

That conflict forces cannabis operators to carry expenses that other industries would consider absurd. Before selling its first product, a business may need lawyers, specialized accountants, security systems, product tracking, laboratory testing, local permits and several layers of state and municipal approval. None of those protections are automatically unreasonable, but together they can create a financial wall that only well-funded companies can climb.

A large corporation may have enough money to survive years of delays, changing regulations and legal expenses. A small independent operator may be paying rent on a building that is not yet allowed to open. A legacy grower who understands the plant may still lack the capital, property or political connections necessary to enter the legal market. Social-equity applicants may receive permission to apply but not the financial support needed to turn a license into an operating business.

Consumers feel the broken system too. Legal cannabis can cost substantially more because taxes, testing and compliance expenses are added at every stage. When regulated products become too expensive, some consumers remain in the unlicensed market. Lawmakers then blame illicit sellers without asking whether the legal system was designed to compete with them.

A regulated market must protect public health. Products should be tested. Labels should be accurate. Businesses should follow reasonable safety standards. But regulation becomes self-defeating when it makes legal participation nearly impossible for small businesses and financially unattractive to consumers. If the legal market cannot compete on price, availability or convenience, prohibition has not really been replaced. It has simply been rearranged.

Then there is the cultural extraction.

People who were arrested, fired, evicted or separated from their families because of cannabis now watch corporations package the same plant as luxury wellness. The language changed. The packaging became cleaner. The stores became brighter. Suddenly, something once used to justify raids and prison sentences is being sold beside yoga mats, skin-care products and expensive candles.

That transition is not justice by itself.

Legalization without expungement leaves criminal records intact. Legalization without access to ownership allows new investors to profit from a market created through someone else’s punishment. Legalization without respect for legacy growers treats history as a branding opportunity. The culture becomes something companies borrow for advertising while the people who built that culture remain on the outside looking in.

None of this means legalization has failed completely. Tested products are better than mystery products. Adults should not be arrested for possessing cannabis. Patients should not have to search underground for medicine. Legal businesses create jobs, provide public revenue and give consumers safer choices. These are real accomplishments.

The mistake is pretending that passing a legalization law completes the work.

A functional cannabis system needs reasonable taxes, accessible licensing and enough room for businesses of different sizes to survive. It needs support that helps qualified social-equity applicants open their doors, not programs that simply hand them paperwork and wish them luck. It needs enforcement aimed at genuinely dangerous conduct—not a return to drug-war tactics under a different name.

It needs automatic expungement wherever possible. People should not have to hire an attorney and fight through another complicated process to clear a conviction for conduct that is no longer illegal. If the government can locate people to arrest them, fine them and imprison them, it can locate their records to erase them.

The system also needs independent testing, accurate labeling and rules that protect consumers without handing the entire market to whoever can afford the most attorneys. Public safety and fair competition should not be treated as opposing goals. A well-designed market should be able to provide both.

Most importantly, the cannabis conversation needs honesty.

Cannabis is neither a harmless miracle nor a social plague. It is a plant with medical potential, adult-use risks, deep cultural meaning and enormous economic value. A mature legal system should be able to hold all four truths without turning any one of them into propaganda.

Legalization opened the gate, but opening a gate is not the same as building a road. Right now, that road has tolls, traps and special lanes reserved for people with money. If lawmakers want the legal market to work, they must stop measuring success only by the number of states that have legalized cannabis or the amount of tax revenue being collected.

Measure the arrests erased.

Measure the small businesses surviving.

Measure whether patients have reliable and affordable access.

Measure product safety, accurate labeling and consumer trust.

Measure whether communities damaged by prohibition have ownership in what came next.

Measure how many legacy growers were given a genuine path into the legal market instead of being pushed aside by corporations with deeper pockets.

Until those numbers matter as much as sales and tax revenue, cannabis may be legal—but the system surrounding it will still feel broken.
